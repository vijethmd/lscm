Lecture 12S1–S16

Reverse Logistics

  • Reverse logistics is traditional logistics with the flow in the opposite direction, moving waste and returns. S3
  • Other names: retro logistics, reverse supply chain, reverse flow, backward supply chain, returns management, aftermarket logistics, aftermarket supply chain. S3
  • Defined as taking waste and returns along the supply chain and reworking them to create a new product that can be sold (Stanisławski & Szymonik, 2023). S3
AuthorDefinition S5–S6
Lambert and Stock"Going the wrong way on a one way street" because most shipments flow one way.
Carter and EllramThe return, upstream movement of a good or material from reuse, recycling or disposal, often done by new, auxiliary channel members.
Thierry et al.Reverse logistics under the umbrella of product recovery management.
Rogers and Tibben-LembkeAdapted from the Council of Logistics Management (below).
Rogers and Tibben-Lembke · exact words
"The process of planning, implementing, and controlling the efficient, cost-effective flow of raw materials, in-process inventory, finished goods and related information from the point of consumption to the point of origin for the purpose of recapturing value or proper disposal." S6

Reverse vs forward, reverse vs green

  • Forward logistics moves raw materials and components for production; reverse logistics handles everything coming from the consumer as waste or return (via recovery facilities, consolidation and collection centres). S7
  • Equating reverse logistics with green logistics is a mistake: green logistics is about environmentally friendly resources. S8

Types of product returns 5 items

TypeExamples (Aït-Kadi et al., 2013) S10
Return of unused productsRecovered electronics, recycled carpets, shoes; white and brown goods (Netherlands); ink cartridge collection
Commercial returnsRetailer surplus, clothing, cosmetics, mail-order returns
Product warranty returnsDefective appliances, spare parts
Wastes and derivative productsPharmaceutical industry, steel components
PackagingPallets, crates, bottles; "Green dot system" (Germany)

Why reverse logistics is important

Government policy and legislationNetherlands (Jan 1999): makers and importers of white and brown goods must take back products; similar rule for cars. Germany: Packaging Ordinance, firms collect all sales packaging. S11
Economic considerationsUS landfill tipping fee rose from $8 to about $49.78 (1985–2010), "about 600%". Recovery is profitable for car parts, copiers, computers, tyres, aviation equipment. S12
Shift to buying servicesMaintenance contracts covering repairs and parts make it easier to take back end-of-life products. S13
Environmental considerationsCuts cost, ensures sustainability, helps meet legislation, lets firms present themselves as "green". S13
Effective waste managementE-waste: 80% of mobile phones are upgraded every two years. Sources: households, industries, institutions. Informal recycling common in India and South Africa. S14

Reverse Logistics (contd.)

Key aspects 6 items

  • Product returns: receive, inspect, refurbish, repackage (defects, damage, overstock, dissatisfaction). S19
  • Remanufacturing and refurbishment: disassemble, repair, clean, reassemble. S19
  • Recycling and disposal: recycle packaging, extract materials, dispose of hazardous waste responsibly. S20
  • Warranty and service management. S20
  • Asset recovery and remarketing: resell, liquidate excess, repurpose. S21
  • Supply chain optimization: RL software, better routes, integrate with forward logistics. S21

The reverse logistics process

GatekeepingControls entry into the RL system; rejects returns that don't belong to the business or that it has no contractual or legal obligation for. Leads to acceptance or rejection. S25
CollectionRecovery and transport of returned products, by customers, third parties or businesses. S26
Collection case: CanonInk cartridge collection program since 1990; pre-paid shipping coupon in new cartridge packs; now in 24 countries. S27
SortingValidates gatekeeping information; sorts by condition and reason for return. S28
Processing / treatmentOptions: repackage, repair, disassemble, reconfigure, remanufacture, upgrade, recycle, donate, sell on secondary markets, dispose. Remanufacturing restores products to a like-new state and replaces more modules than repair. S29
DisposalLandfill or incinerator, the last resort; remove hazardous materials; incinerators should recover heat for electricity. S30

Visibility and transparency · efficient returns management · product recovery and disposition · collaboration with partners · technology and data analytics · reverse logistics network design · compliance and regulation · customer experience · continuous improvement and innovation · sustainability and corporate responsibility. Case studies

  • Amazon: no-fuss returns, RL training programs, full control of a product's life. NRF: a record $761 billion of merchandise returned in 2021; 16.6% of holiday sales returned; online return rate nearly 21% (18% in 2020); Amazon net sales $469 billion. S47–S48
  • Amazon donates eligible returns to charities in North America, Europe, Australia, Japan. S49 SIPP packaging for a Samsung 49" TV removes 9 components, cuts package volume 69% and air shipped 87%. S50
  • IKEA initiatives: generous returns policy, refurbishment centres, material recovery facilities (wood, metal, plastics), circular design (durability, repairability, recyclability). Goal: carbon neutral by 2030. S51–S54

Green Logistics

  • GSCM reflects the relationship between the natural environment and supply chain operations, and measures supply chain performance on environmental aspects. S60
  • GSCM practices (cycle): green purchasing, production, warehousing, distribution, transportation, raw material, design. S61
Green transportationDriven by freight CO₂ since the 1990s; up to 71% in 2016, expected to rise 50% more by 2050. S62
Green procurementBuying products and services less harmful to the environment at competitive prices. S62
Green warehouseAdequate stock levels to avoid material waste and transport cost. S62
Green designEcological design: clean, safe factory, lower disposal cost. S63
Green manufacturingAlso ecological production: little or no pollution or waste. S63
Green marketingAlso ecological, social, environmental or sustainable marketing. S63
Green logistics · exact words
"Green logistics, also known as eco-friendly logistics, refers to the practice of managing the flow of goods and resources in a manner that minimizes environmental impact." S64

Initiatives: alternative fuels, route optimization, energy-efficient vehicles, less packaging waste, reverse logistics for recycling, eco-friendly packaging. S64

  • Before the 1960s there was little concern for environmental degradation; the environment's capacity was seen as infinite (Murphy & Poist, 1995). Green logistics merges research strands from the past 40 years. S65

Challenges and technologies

  • Challenges: rising raw material prices; COVID-19 sourcing disruptions; changing management models toward "energy conservation, environmental protection, and low consumption". S70
  • Technologies: green transport (green vehicle routing counting CO₂-equivalent); logistics solutions for carbon neutrality (green procurement, packaging, warehousing; the "last-mile challenge"); horizontal collaboration (pooling logistics between two or more manufacturers). S72

Case studies

  • DHL: operates in 220 countries; founded 1969 by Adrian Dalsey, Larry Hillblom and Robert Lynn; sustainability and CSR focus since 2009; GoGreen program; aim to cut carbon emissions 50% and pollution 70%; bicycles and electric vehicles for pickup and delivery. S81–S82
  • Myntra: plastic-free packaging (recycled paper bags, shredded carton instead of bubble wrap, paper tape, Kraft paper invoice pouches, RFID-tagged multi-use polyester bags); 670 tonnes of plastic diverted by Sept 2021; "e-commerce-ready packaging" (no secondary layer); partnered with Canopy and the Better Cotton Initiative; two solar-powered fulfilment centres. S83–S85
  • IKEA green transport: founder's line: any designer can make a $1,000 desk, but a sustainable, beautiful one for $50 is marvelling. Staff travel plans (bicycles, walking, buses, car sharing); loading shelves replace wooden pallets; transport emissions 19.4% (2019 report); target 30% GHG cut from transport in 2020 and 70% by 2030. S86–S87
  • Future: BEVs suit short city routes; fuel cell vehicles (FCV) suit longer routes of large trucks and buses; DEA (data envelopment analysis) evaluates green practices; city infrastructure and supplier selection matter. S88–S89
Lecture 15S97–S127

Lean Logistics

Lean logistics · Paul Myerson (2012)
"Lean Logistics is an approach to supply chain management that focuses on eliminating waste, improving efficiency, streamlining processes, creating constant flow, enhancing collaboration, and achieving quality." S100

Alan Harrison (2011): a way to recognise and eliminate wasteful activities to increase product flow and speed. S100

  • Benefits: improved efficiency, increased profitability, better customer service, reduced costs. S102
  • History: from lean manufacturing in Japan; the term was coined by John Krafcik in his 1988 MIT master's thesis; lean manufacturing was pioneered by Toyota engineer Taiichi Ohno in the 1970s. S103
  • Logistics costs are 5–15% of turnover in industry and 15–25% in retail. S104

The 3Ms

Muda · wasteAny activity that adds no value. S106
Muri · overburdenUnnecessary stress on people and processes, caused by Mura and poor training, unclear methods, wrong tools. Leads to breakdowns and absenteeism. S113–S114
Mura · unevennessInconsistency. "Mura drives Muda". Example: the month-end rush that produces a hockey-stick production graph. S116–S117

7 types of muda (categorized by Taiichi Ohno in the Toyota Production System): transport, inventory, motion, waiting, overproduction, over-processing, defects. S108–S110

  • Other wastes: talent (unused skills), resources (lights and machines left on), by-products. Tools to remove waste: Poka-Yoke, Kanban, Takt time, SMED, one-piece flow. S111
  • Common waste areas: warehousing ("always wasteful, even if sometimes unavoidable"), trucks (motion), travel time, people, e-vehicles (part of the solution), inventory. S112

Principles of lean logistics 6 items

Eliminating waste · improving efficiency · streamlining processes · creating constant flow · enhancing collaboration · achieving quality. S118–S120

Lean logistics KPIs

  • On-time delivery, lead time, inventory turnover, order accuracy, transportation cost per unit, warehouse utilization, cycle time, dock-to-stock time, return rate, order fulfilment cycle time. S122–S123
  • Perfect order rate: orders delivered with no errors (accurate, complete, intact). Backorder rate: orders not fillable immediately. Supplier on-time delivery, order lead time variability, shipment damage rate, sustainability metrics, employee engagement. S124–S125

Lean Logistics: Methodologies and Value Stream Mapping

Lean logistics methodologies 13 items

Value stream mapping · JIT inventory · standardized work · continuous improvement culture (Kaizen events) · supplier collaboration · Lean Six Sigma (DMAIC, root cause analysis) · technology integration (WMS, TMS) · sustainable packaging · route optimization · demand forecasting · performance-based contracts with LSPs · modular transport (intermodal, containerization) · reverse logistics optimization and performance monitoring. S130–S134

Value stream mapping (VSM)

  • Maps the whole value stream from customer request to delivery to find waste and bottlenecks; defines value from the customer's perspective. S135
  • Deming: "If you can't describe what you are doing as a process, you don't know what you're doing." S135
  • Current-state VSM + lean basics = future-state VSM. S136
1. PrepareCase for change, scope (start and end), requirements, measurements, event logistics. S137
2. Gather dataDevelop the current state: walk the value stream, record lead time, cycle time, defects, batch sizes, WIP; map item and information flow. S138
3. Future state & action plansFuture-state map, action plans, timeline. Action plan template: step, problem, person responsible, actions, due date, metrics. S139–S140
4. Execute to planWorkshops and projects; continuous improvement environment. S141
5. AlignPeriodic value stream alignment meetings to review results and update the plan. S142
The stapler example S143–S144

Pick up paper 1 s · walk to stapler 5 s, 20 ft · staple 2 s · walk back 5 s, 20 ft · put down 1 s. Total 14 s, 40 ft; value-added time 4 s (28%), value-added distance 0. The walking is "excess travel".

Lean Logistics: DMAIC, JIT, 5S, Kaizen, SMED and Toyota

DMAIC

DefineTeam and sponsor agree on scope, goals and financial targets. S148
MeasureCurrent-state performance, verified with VSM and capability measures. S148
AnalyzeRoot causes of deviation from customer requirements. S148
ImproveDesign improvements, pilot new processes; new KPIs, future-state maps, control plans. S149
ControlMonitoring systems and control plans to sustain results. S149

JIT

  • Produce goods only as needed: less storage cost, excess inventory and obsolescence risk; needs close supplier coordination. S150
  • JIT continuous improvement cycle (8 steps): Design (process) → Manage (TQM) → Pull (Kanban, smaller lots) → Establish (vendor relationships) → Fine-tune (inventory) → Build (skills) → Refine (product and process) → Review (KPIs, root cause). S151–S153

5S, Kaizen, SMED

  • 5S: Sort (Seiri), Set in order (Seiton), Shine (Seiso), Standardize (Seiketsu), Sustain (Shitsuke). S154–S155
  • Kaizen = Kai (change) + Zen (better) = continuous improvement: "Everybody! Everyday! Everywhere!" Small improvements are faster, lower risk, and add up to more than one big change. S156 S173
  • Kaizen examples: easy to lubricate, easy to inspect, tyre changeover cut from 5–10 minutes to 5–10 seconds. S157
  • SMED (single-minute exchange of die): changeovers in under 10 minutes ("single-digit minute"). OTED (one-touch exchange of die): under 100 seconds. S158

Challenges in implementing lean 8 items

Cultural resistance (the biggest) · lack of leadership commitment · silo mentality · limited employee engagement · inadequate training · measurement and accountability · sustainability of gains · external factors. S159–S161

Case: Toyota Production System (TPS)

  • Toyota: HQ Toyota, Aichi; founder Kiichiro Toyoda; TPS founders Taiichi Ohno and Shigeo Shingo. S163
  • 7 TPS principles: reduced setup time, small-lot production, employee involvement, quality at the source, equipment maintenance, pull production, supplier involvement. S164
  • Batch & push: 21 min for the first piece, 30+ min for the order. Continuous flow ("make one, move one"): 3 min first piece, 12 min total. S167
  • "Just-in-Time" was coined by Kiichiro Toyoda in 1937; after WWII Taiichi Ohno moved from Toyoda Spinning and Weaving to drive JIT and Jidoka. S168
  • Jidoka principles: discover an abnormality, stop the process, fix the immediate problem, investigate and solve the root cause. Poka-yoke: mistake-proofing; low-cost devices that detect abnormalities or stop the machine before defects are made. S169–S170
  • GEMBA: Genchi Genbutsu (go and see), Engage (5 whys), Muda/Mura/Muri, Be respectful, Analyze. S171
  • Heijunka: production smoothing to level fluctuating demand. S172
  • Standardized work has three elements: takt time, working sequence, standard in-process stock. S174
  • Per-head production rose from 39.7 to 70.8 vehicles (about 80%) after TPS. S176
  • US market share: GM 40% vs Toyota 2% (1970); Toyota 3% (1980), 8% (1990), 9% (2000), 13% in 2006 while GM fell to 26%. Japanese word jojo: "slowly, gradually, and steadily". S178
