Lecture 58S1–S33

Sustainable Supply Chain

  • Supply chain sustainability: considering the environmental and human impact of a product's journey from raw materials to delivery; minimize harm from energy, water and waste while helping people and communities, on top of revenue and profit. S3
Sustainable development · Brundtland Commission (UN) S5
"development that meets the needs of the present without compromising the ability of future generations to meet their own needs."

The 2005 UN World Summit named economic, environmental and social sustainability as the "three pillars"; all three must be reconciled.

  • Growth of Brazil, China, India raises living standards but pressures resources. Three drivers of focus: reduce risk and improve financial performance, attract customers who value sustainability, make the world more sustainable. S6
  • Unilever: drip irrigation for Brazilian tomato growers; almost half of sales from emerging economies; buys about 10% of the world's tea and 30% of all spinach. S7
  • Wal-Mart: started as a defensive move; efficient bulbs, skylights, less packaging; with Costco redesigned the one-gallon milk jug, saving 10–20 cents a gallon. S8

Triple Bottom Line (TBL)

  • Coined by John Elkington; a yardstick for how well a chain builds sustainability while staying profitable. Aims: transparency, economic opportunity, local competitiveness, less environmental degradation. S9
  • Metrics: social (health, gender equality, labour rights), environmental (certified suppliers, fossil fuel, waste, electricity), economic (financial flow, ROI, investments). S10
  • Benefits: lower carbon footprint and energy cost, better brand and productivity, future growth and financial security. S11
  • Five business areas that benefit: supply chain operations, branding (MIT Sloan: consumers may pay 2–10% more for transparent products), compliance (UN 2030 SDGs), investor relations (nearly half of investors interested in sustainable funds, Gallup), corporate culture (millennials). S12–S13

10 key sustainability metrics

Carbon footprint · energy consumption · water usage · waste generation · supplier diversity · ethical sourcing · supplier compliance (Fair Trade, FSC) · product lifecycle analysis · transportation efficiency (MPG, kg CO₂ per ton-km) · social impact. S14–S15

Drivers that add to sustainability

FacilitiesWalmart's energy-efficient stores; waste management turned into profit. S17
InventoryTrack landfill inventory; Walmart cut phosphates in detergents and redesigned packaging. S17
TransportationWalmart cut delivery fuel use by 65%; IKEA designs for high volume and weight density. S17
SourcingStarbucks' C.A.F.E. program; Mattel's lead-paint toy recall shows the reputational risk. S18
InformationNo standard reporting; Walmart's packaging scorecard. S18
PricingTime-of-day pricing; tax credits (e.g. Nissan Leaf); customers' willingness to pay drives long-term change. S18

7 ways to improve sustainability

  1. Find pain points: calculate the product carbon footprint (PCF) first. S20
  2. Optimize transportation: it was about 29–30% of US GHG emissions in 2019 (largest sector; electricity 25%, industry 23%); fill trucks (3D load software), EVs, route optimization. S20–S21
  3. Collaborate with sustainable suppliers: standards GRI 408 and 409, SASB human capital, ISO 14001; third-party verified ESG reports and PCFs. S22
  4. Minimize inventory: warehouses are about 13% of SC-related GHG emissions. S23
  5. Reuse waste or by-products (bottle deposit schemes). S23
  6. Eco-friendly packaging: only 1% of plastics are biodegradable (EU); the Higg index gives material-level GHG data. S24
  7. Nudge regulators and consumers: Starbucks discounts for own cups, aiming to cut single-use plastic 50%. S25
  • Challenges: cost is the primary barrier (worse for small firms), finding sustainable components, inherited supply chains from acquisitions, organization structure, lack of customer interest. S26
  • Trends: circular economy (eliminate waste, design for reuse) and data-driven supply chains (cloud, IoT). S27
  • EY case: 8 in 10 executives are increasing sustainability efforts, but only about 4 in 10 have seen better visibility. 55% expect better operational risk management in 1–3 years (20% already); 31% already have better efficiency (51% expect it); 54% expect higher share price in 3–5 years. Five actions include aligning with commitments, visibility and traceability, wider ROI measurement, tax incentives and grants. S28–S29
Lecture 59S34–S68

Emergency Supply Chain

  • The Joint Logistics Enterprise defines a complex emergency as international conflict, humanitarian and domestic disaster relief scenarios combining warfare, food insecurity, epidemics and social conflict. S36
  • Emergency supply chain: a specialized framework for urgent, unforeseen situations (disasters, health emergencies, geopolitical crises): rapid mobilization, coordination, deployment of goods. Two domains: military expeditions and non-governmental disaster relief. S37

Why a separate framework

  • A disruption hits an existing chain whose identity (stakeholders, products, facilities) stays the same. An emergency supply chain is formed in response to the emergency, often without prior planning. S41–S42
  • Emergency chains prioritize effectiveness over efficiency: an "at all cost" approach; fly supplies instead of sealift; more less-than-truckload shipments. Conventional chains maximize service at minimum cost. S43–S44

Uniqueness of emergency logistics 4 items

  1. Diverse, immediate services needed (water, food, medicine, shelter); timing drives survival rates. S45
  2. Real-time demand information is hard to get (affected people can't report). S46
  3. Service efficiency over profit, e.g. costly air transport. S46
  4. Collaboration among government, public bodies, firms, individuals. S46
  • Solutions: governments set up an emergency SCM system in normal times; invest in labour, R&D, IT and software; strengthen communication to cut information distortion and the bullwhip effect; collaborative mechanisms like electronic procurement hubs. S47–S48

Humanitarian supply chain management

  • Buying and delivering requested supplies and services where and when needed, with best value for money, after an emergency. Technical side: planning, information, warehousing, pre-positioning, transport, distribution. Strategic side: decisions, coordination, partnerships. S49
  • Examples: 2015 Nepal earthquake (helicopters), Hurricane Maria, Puerto Rico 2017 (power grid restoration), 2010 Haiti earthquake (pre-positioned supplies), Za'atari refugee camp, Jordan, sub-Saharan healthcare. S50–S57
  • Challenges: unpredictable demand (Ebola in West Africa), limited access (Rohingya camps in Bangladesh), domestic barriers (Syrian crisis border crossings); also lack of trust, many organizations, inefficient collaboration, fraud, centralization, cost inefficiencies. S52–S55
  • 60–80% of the cost of an emergency response is within the supply chain; 30 humanitarian organizations spend about $6 billion a year on supply chains. S57
  • General steps: needs assessment → planning → sourcing → procurement → transportation → warehousing → distribution, plus monitoring. S58–S59
  • WFP elements: planning, sourcing, logistics, efficiency, effectiveness, with information along the chain. Cargo: relief items (medical, shelter, WASH, dangerous goods, equipment, food) and food commodities (cereals, oils, salt, sugar, pulses, fortified foods, RTUF). S60–S61

Case: Australia's risky food supply chain

  • Floods in Queensland and New South Wales emptied shelves; supermarkets introduced buying limits; the IPCC warns extreme weather will become more frequent. S63
  • Governments rely mainly on the food industry for resilience and on charities for emergency relief; Brisbane Markets closed, Pacific Highway blocked. Melbourne research identifies features of a resilient food system (closed-loop agriculture, circular economy, decentralized and local chains). S64–S66
Lecture 60S69–S97

Assets Management in Supply Chain

  • A supply chain asset is any tangible or intangible resource that contributes to the chain's functioning: facilities, warehouses, vehicles, inventory, information systems, IP, supplier relationships, brand. S71
  • Supply chain assets are those directly attributable to supply, manufacture and distribution; they exclude cash, cash equivalents and liquid investments. S72
  • Asset management (Corporate Finance Institute): developing, operating, maintaining and selling assets cost-effectively; includes scheduled maintenance, work orders, historical data, scheduling and institutional knowledge. Reactive-only management stalls productivity. S74–S75
Inventory managementAsset management S77
Tracks stock entering and leavingManages items the firm keeps for their whole life cycle, procurement to disposal

Returns only if customers are dissatisfiedUsers borrow and return items to the asset pool
Minimal detail per itemDecides which assets add value and which are liabilities
  • Types: raw materials, WIP, finished goods, packaging, tools and equipment, vehicles, warehousing, IT assets, intellectual property, human resources. S78–S79

Asset management KPIs

MTBFMean time between failures = (time in use − unplanned downtime) ÷ number of breakdowns. S80
MTTRMean time to repair = total maintenance time ÷ number of repairs; compares technicians. S80
OEEOverall Equipment Effectiveness = Availability × Performance × Quality; availability = true ÷ planned operating time; performance = actual ÷ planned cycle time; quality = valid ÷ total pieces. S80
OthersAsset utilization rate, ROA, maintenance cost per asset, downtime, availability, life cycle cost, performance ratio, work order completion rate, Asset Condition Index (ACI). S81

Asset tracking

  • End-to-end tracking gives cost-effective monitoring, real-time visibility, traceability (cold chains), sustainability optimization, control. Not adopting it risks inefficiency, disruptions, asset loss and losing competitiveness. S82–S83
  • Technologies: RFID, GPS, barcodes, IoT sensors, NFC, BLE (indoor), RTLS (real-time location systems), cloud software, blockchain, AI/ML, mobile apps, drones, edge computing, AR, voice recognition, thermal imaging, ultrasound (indoor where GPS fails), smart packaging, telematics (fleet), wearables. S84–S87
  • Best practices: comprehensive inventory of assets, standardized tracking, centralized database, regular audits, maintenance protocols, real-time monitoring, preventive maintenance, lifecycle management, vendor management, training, analytics, continuous improvement. S88–S89
  • Future trends (11): IoT, predictive maintenance, blockchain, AI, autonomous vehicles and drones, AR for maintenance, digital twins, sustainable asset management, cloud systems, robotics, multi-tier visibility. S90–S91

Case: EAM in Microsoft Dynamics 365

  • Asset Management announced in the October 2019 release; Microsoft ERP solutions: D365 Finance, D365 Supply Chain Management, D365 Business Central. S92–S93

Worked example (my own numbers, using the slide formulas)

Availability 90%, performance 95%, quality 98% → OEE = 0.90 × 0.95 × 0.98 ≈ 83.8%. A machine used 1,000 h with 40 h unplanned downtime and 4 breakdowns → MTBF = 960 ÷ 4 = 240 h; 4 repairs taking 20 h → MTTR = 5 h.
