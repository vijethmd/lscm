Lecture 26S1–S25

Supply Chain Uncertainty and Processes

  • Hook: are you 100% sure the 1,500-unit truck arrives this weekend? "Too much inventory kicks off finance; not enough inventory takes off your customers." S3
  • Supply chain uncertainty: unpredictability and volatility that disrupt the flow of goods, from natural disasters, geopolitics, labour shortages, logistics disruptions, demand swings. S4

Demand vs implied demand uncertainty

  • Demand uncertainty: overall uncertainty of customer demand (trends, behaviour, economy). S5
  • Implied demand uncertainty: uncertainty for the portion of demand the supply chain plans to satisfy, given order frequency, lead times and service levels. S5
  • A firm serving emergency orders faces higher implied uncertainty than one supplying the same product with long lead time. Raising the service level raises implied demand uncertainty, even if demand uncertainty is unchanged. S6

Supply uncertainty

  • Driven by supply chain capability (e.g. low yields with new PC components) and the life-cycle position of the product: new phones have high supply uncertainty, salt in a supermarket low. S7–S8

Countering uncertainty

Diversify suppliers · enhance visibility · build flexibility (buffer stock, agile manufacturing) · strengthen relationships · risk management practices · invest in resilience · monitor and adapt; plus automation, AI and analytics. S9–S11

Uncertainty vs risk S13

Risk is a function of outcome and probability, so it can be estimated. Uncertainty occurs when decision makers cannot estimate the outcome of an event or its probability.

Type (Michel Wang, Australian courier industry)Sources S14–S16

Mitigating uncertainty through automated planning 5 items

  • Regular plan generation: moving plan reviews from yearly to weekly can cut inventory by 37%. S17
  • Consistency in planning; integration of more data sources; visibility across the chain (reduces the bullwhip effect); holistic KPI tracking. S18–S19

Lecture 27S26–S53

Supply Chain Operational Views

SCOR model

  • Supply Chain Operations Reference (SCOR) model: the planning and diagnostic system of SCM, from the Supply-Chain Council (a global non-profit consortium). S29
  • Five processes: Plan, Source, Make, Deliver, Return. Five performance dimensions: reliability, responsiveness, agility, costs, asset management efficiency. S29–S30
  • Elements, the 4Ps: Performance (KPIs), Process (plan, source, make, deliver, return, enable), Practice (methods and tools), People (skills, training). S32–S33
  • More than 250 metrics, summarized as reliability (right quantity and condition), responsiveness (speed), costs, asset management (working capital, fixed assets). S34
  • Advantages: whole-chain scope, benchmarking, metric alignment across boundaries, common vocabulary, standardized improvement. S35
  • Disadvantages: complexity, one-size-fits-all, lack of flexibility (linear), limited scope (no social responsibility or sustainability), dependence on data. S36
  • Example: e-commerce, where digital processes are well documented and easy to analyse with SCOR. S37–S38

Cycle view

  • Processes split into cycles, each at the interface between two successive stages; defines roles and owners. Useful for operational decisions. S39 S41
CycleInterfaceSteps S44–S47
Customer orderCustomer – retailerCustomer arrival → order entry → order fulfilment → order receiving
ReplenishmentRetailer – distributorRetail order trigger → entry → fulfilment → receiving
ManufacturingDistributor – manufacturerOrder arrival → production scheduling → manufacturing and shipping → receiving
ProcurementManufacturer – supplierOrder per production schedule → supplier scheduling → component manufacturing and shipping → receiving

Push/pull view

  • Pull: executed in response to a customer order (reactive). Push: executed in anticipation of orders (speculative). S48
Lecture 28S54–S88

Supply Chain Models and Modelling Systems

  • Supply chain modelling: designing and analysing a supply chain to find improvements and simulate scenarios. Models include static, dynamic and Monte Carlo simulation. Benefits: lower costs, efficiency, customer satisfaction. S56–S57
  • 8 major components: inventory (the most critical, hits the bottom line), transportation, warehousing, manufacturing, procurement, distribution, customer service, IT. S58–S59
  • Model types for decisions: linear programming (optimize resource distribution), simulation, queuing, statistical. Choose by the optimization goal. S60

Types of supply chain models

ModelKey idea · example
ContinuousSteady flow, JIT, stable demand; less flexible. Amazon. S62–S63
FastQuick delivery, short life cycles. Zara (new trends within weeks vs six months). S65
Efficient chainReduce waste in highly competitive markets; relies on accurate production predictions; vulnerable to labour or material shortages. S66–S67
AgileResponsive to change; specialist goods; profitable only up to a volume threshold. Four traits: process alignment, virtual integration, network base, market sensitivity. Nike. S69–S70
Custom-configuredCustomized to customer demand; mixes agile and continuous flow. Dell. S71
FlexibleHandles peaks and long low periods; needs part segmentation, precise stocking algorithms, flexible planning. Hewlett Packard. S72
Virtual (future)Cloud and analytics. Procter & Gamble. S73
  • Best practices: understand needs, choose the approach, validate with accurate data, test "what if" scenarios, consider several models, involve stakeholders, update, document assumptions, train, monitor. S74–S75
  • Challenges: complex relationships, imperfect data, multiple objectives, dynamic environment, integration, resource constraints, scenario complexity, risk, change management. S76–S77
  • Supply chain control tower: a cloud-based solution using ML, AI and IoT for end-to-end, real-time visibility; the future of SC modelling. S78

Case studies

  • Intel Atom: supply chain cost about $5.50 per chip was fine for $100 chips but not for a $20 chip. Only lever: inventory. Switched to make-to-order (pilot in Malaysia), cut the test window from 5 days to a biweekly 2-day process, added S&OP and VMI. Order cycle fell from nine weeks to two; cost cut by more than $4 per unit. S80–S82
  • Chemical company: fragmented network after acquisitions; a large MILP (mixed-integer linear programming) model → 10% lower costs and next-day delivery for 95% of products. Hurdles: data across ERP systems, scepticism. S83–S85
Lecture 29S89–S120

Supply Chain Drivers: Facilities, Inventory, Transportation

  • Supply chain drivers shape responsiveness and efficiency: facilities, inventory, transportation, information, sourcing, pricing. S91–S93
  • Cross-functional drivers have broader scope, rely on internal collaboration, and impact responsiveness and agility more. Logistics drivers focus on physical movement, external partners, efficiency and cost. S96–S97

Facilities

  • Physical places where inventory is stored or transformed. Centralizing raises efficiency but cuts responsiveness; decentralizing does the reverse. S98
  • Toyota and Honda open plants in every major market for responsiveness; Honda plants could build SUVs and cars, staying busy in the 2008 downturn. S99
  • Decisions: role (flexible vs dedicated capacity; product vs functional focus; cross-docking vs storage), location, capacity (excess capacity = flexible but costly). S100
  • Metrics: capacity, utilization, processing/setup/down/idle time, cost per unit, quality losses, theoretical and actual flow time, flow time efficiency (theoretical ÷ actual), product variety, volume share of top 20% SKUs, batch size, production service level. S101–S102

Inventory

  • Role: matches supply and demand, exploits economies of scale, increases responsiveness, but high levels raise costs. S103–S104
  • Little's law links inventory (I), flow time (T) and throughput (D): I = D × T. Less inventory at the same throughput means shorter flow time. S104
  • Amazon: best-sellers in many regional warehouses; slow movers in few; slowest printed on demand or ordered from the publisher. S106
  • Components: level of product availability, cycle inventory (usual demand), safety inventory (buffer), seasonal inventory (peaks). S107
  • Metrics: cash conversion cycle, average inventory, seasonal inventory, inventory turnover (COGS or sales ÷ average inventory), DIO (average inventory ÷ COGS per day), batch size, average safety inventory, fill rate, fraction of time out of stock, obsolete inventory. S108–S110

Transportation

  • Faster modes improve responsiveness but cost more. Dell flies components from Asia to keep inventory low. S112–S113
  • Blue Nile: FedEx overnight delivery plus centralized diamond inventory, no physical stores. S114
  • Decisions: network design (direct or via consolidation points), mode (air, truck, rail, sea, pipeline, internet). Inbound transport hits COGS; outbound hits SG&A. S115
  • Metrics: average inbound/outbound transport cost, shipment size, cost per shipment, fraction transported by mode. S116–S117
  • Trade-off: air/express = responsive; sea/rail = efficient. S118
Lecture 30S121–S150

Supply Chain Drivers: Information, Sourcing, Pricing

Information

  • Role: asset utilization, coordination of flows, responsiveness, lower costs. Examples: Seven-Eleven Japan, Wal-Mart. S123–S125
  • Share the minimum information necessary; beyond a point, marginal cost exceeds marginal benefit. S127
  • Sunsweet Growers (dried fruit, California) replaced Excel with an S&OP suite: production overruns fell from 30% to under 15%, forecast accuracy up 15–20%, warnings 2–3 weeks earlier. S128
  • Decisions: push vs pull, coordination and sharing, S&OP (align sales forecasts with production and inventory), enabling technologies (ERP, analytics, cloud). S129
  • Metrics: forecast horizon (≥ decision lead time), update frequency, forecast error, seasonal factors, variance from plan, ratio of demand variability to order variability (below 1 suggests a bullwhip effect). S130–S131
  • Trade-off: complexity vs value; sharing aggregate sales may beat detailed POS data. S132

Sourcing

  • Buying goods and services; deciding responsive vs efficient sources, in-house vs third party. IKEA sources low-cost modules; Dell sources some PCs from China. S133
  • Outsource next-day delivery to package carriers; Zara keeps quick response in-house. S134
  • Cisco: outsources almost all manufacturing. Low-end home routers made in China for efficiency; high-end products from US contract manufacturers for responsiveness. S135
  • Decisions: in-house or outsource, supplier selection (negotiation or auction), procurement. S136
  • Metrics: days payable outstanding, average purchase price, supply quality, supply lead time, on-time deliveries, price range, purchase quantity, supplier reliability. S137–S138
  • Trade-off: increase the supply chain surplus; outsource only if it raises total surplus more than doing it in-house. S139

Pricing

  • Sets revenue and shapes demand; short-term discounts can shift demand to match an inflexible supply. S140
  • Costco: consistently low prices for a segment that values affordability over availability. S141
  • Amazon, July 2008, two books worth $30: standard 3–5 days $4.98; two-day $13.97; one-day $22.97; free shipping 7–14 days. Free-shipping customers level out warehouse workload. S142
  • Decisions: pricing and economies of scale (quantity discounts), everyday low pricing vs high-low pricing, fixed vs menu pricing. S143
  • Metrics: profit margin, days sales outstanding, incremental fixed cost per order, incremental variable cost per unit, average sale price, average order size, range of sale price, range of periodic sales. S144–S146
  • Trade-off: increase firm profits; EDLP stabilizes demand. S147
Wrap-up S148

Financial measures: ROE, ROA, accounts payable turnover, profit margin, asset turnover, receivable turnover, inventory turns, PP&E turns, cash-to-cash cycle. Six drivers create strategic fit by balancing responsiveness and efficiency.
