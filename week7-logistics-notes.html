Supply Chain Impellers and Value Chain

  • Impellers are the driving forces that propel supply chain performance (facilities, inventory, transport, information, sourcing, pricing and more). S3
ImpellerMeaning
Traditional three S4

Developments in IT toolsReal-time tracking and visibility; tools: MRP, ERP, VMI, TAV.
GlobalizationNew markets and consumers, more competition and risk.
Two more in some literature S5
Focus on sustainabilityEthical sourcing, energy-efficient logistics, waste reduction.
Collaboration and integrationAcross internal functions and with external partners.

Four supply chain concepts

Systems conceptThe chain is one interconnected system; optimize the whole, not segments; disruptions ripple. S8
Total cost conceptCount all costs, direct, indirect and hidden, and minimize the total. S9
Trade-off conceptImproving one aspect costs another: cost vs quality, cost vs speed, inventory vs responsiveness. S10
Bullwhip effectSmall consumer demand swings become amplified orders upstream. Causes: information delays, forecast errors, order batching. Cures: better forecasting, shorter lead times, VMI, information sharing. S11

Value chain in a company

  • Primary chain: New product development → marketing and sales → operations → distribution → service. S12–S14
  • Supporting functions: finance, accounting, information technology, human resources. S15

Essential considerations for supply chain success 5 items

  1. Supply sense: anticipate and understand changes in the supply environment. S17
  2. Supply response: operations that make things happen (manufacturing, asset management); adjust supply to change. S17
  3. Deciding and committing: strategic decisions aligned with business goals. S18
  4. Demand sense: sense and anticipate changes in customer demand with data and analytics. S18
  5. Demand response: react quickly by adjusting production, inventory, distribution. S19
Lecture 32S23–S52

Strategic Supply Chain Management

  • A supply chain strategy is an overarching plan for planning, design, execution, control and monitoring of supply chain activities; it must align with business goals. Best-in-class firms have a separate strategy for make-to-stock, make-to-order and engineer-to-order. S25
  • Strategic management process: review the current situation (mission, objectives) → analyse internal and external environment (SWOT) → develop strategies (corporate, business, functional) → implement → evaluate. Formulation = creating; implementation = putting into action. S26
  • Components of SC strategy: sourcing, logistics, production, distribution, customer service. S28
  • 21st-century competition: rapid technological change, global economy, globalization, importance of knowledge and people. Competitive advantage needs deciding both what the firm will do and what it will not do. S30–S31
  • Cost advantage: control cost drivers and reconfigure the value chain (new process, raw material, automation, forward or backward integration, new channel, relocation, direct sales). S33–S34
  • Differentiation advantage: lower buyers' costs, raise performance, create perceptions of uniqueness and reluctance to switch. S35–S36
  • Effective SC strategy is collaborative, agile, resilient, digital, and invests in cybersecurity. S37–S38
  • Optimization: S&OP, workforce management, network design, demand planning, inventory optimization. S39
  • Strategy per value chain component: product development (portfolio, in-house vs outsourced), marketing (segmentation, positioning, pricing, promotion), supply chain (procurement, operations, distribution, outsourcing), design and service strategies. S41–S46

Cases

  • Unilever (CPFR): five-year plan from early 2000; savings of $14.24 billion; merged almost 30 warehouses into 5 hubs; Collaborative Planning, Forecasting and Replenishment with retailers raised forecast accuracy 10%, cut inventory holding 10%, raised sales volume 5%. S47–S48
  • Eaton: resilience in the pandemic via education, multi-sourcing, supplier collaboration. Intel: ethical sourcing with a smelter audit protocol and RFID so tin, tungsten, gold and tantalum are conflict-free. S49
Lecture 33S53–S81

Strategic SCM (contd.): SWOT and Porter

  • External environment → opportunities and threats: what the firm might do. Internal environment → unique resources, capabilities, competencies: what the firm can do. S56–S57
  • Internal analysis: resources (tangible, intangible) → capabilities → core competencies → competitive advantage → strategic competitiveness. Four criteria of sustainable advantage: valuable, rare, costly to imitate, non-substitutable. S58
  • Value is measured by product performance and attributes customers pay for; superior value → above-average returns. Walmart: "everyday low pricing". S59

SWOT

  • Strengths and weaknesses are internal; opportunities and threats are external. S61–S63
  • Supply chain SWOT example: strengths = purchasing power, payment history, quality, core competencies; weaknesses = poor credit, late deliveries, capacity limits; opportunities = more warehouse space, new software; threats = downturns, supplier bankruptcies, rising prices. S63
  • Amazon: fleet, warehouse network, technology. 2,373+ facilities worldwide, 200+ fulfilment centres in North America, about 319 million sq ft of US warehousing. FBA (Fulfilled by Amazon), FBA Onsite, robots, Scout six-wheeled delivery robot. Inventory turnover about 8.39 (FY Dec 2022). S64–S65
  • Amazon SWOT: strengths brand, pioneer, cost advantage; weaknesses low margins, seasonality; threats patent infringement, cybersecurity. S67

Porter's Five Forces

  • Created by Harvard's Michael Porter, published 1979 ("How Competitive Forces Shape Strategy"). S68
  • Applied to supply chains: supplier power (concentration, switching costs), buyer power (concentration, price sensitivity), threat of new entrants (barriers, technology), threat of substitutes, rivalry among competitors. S69–S72

Implementing SC strategy

Identify demand types · understand the product portfolio (different strategies per line) · know customers' locations · manage inventory · know suppliers' locations (foreign suppliers → longer lead times). S74–S75

Case: Starbucks

  • 35,000+ stores, $32 billion+ revenue; vertically integrated, working with about 400,000 coffee producers; C.A.F.E. (Coffee and Farmer Equity) Practices. S76
  • 2008: Peter Gibbons simplified the chain into four macro-operations (plan, source, manufacture, deliver), centralized logistics, and a binary scorecard (safety, on-time supply and fulfilment, total cost, financial performance). S77
  • Results: 98.6% ethically sourced coffee; sales up 5%. S78
Lecture 34S82–S99

Achieving Supply Chain Strategic Fit

  • Competitive strategy defines which customer needs a firm meets and how it stands apart: Wal-Mart (low prices, availability), McMaster-Carr (MRO convenience and responsiveness), Blue Nile vs Zales (variety and low price online vs in-store experience). S84–S85
  • SC strategies: efficient, responsive, lean, agile, collaborative. S87–S88
Strategic fit S89

Consistency between the customer priorities of the competitive strategy and the supply chain capabilities specified by the supply chain strategy; both strategies share the same goals.

Three steps S91: (1) understand the customer and supply chain uncertainty · (2) understand supply chain capabilities · (3) achieve strategic fit (coordinate functional strategies).

Step 1: customer and uncertainty

  • Customer needs: quantity per lot, variety, service level, tolerated response time, price, rate of innovation. S92
  • Implied demand uncertainty increases when: quantity range grows, lead time falls, variety grows, channels grow, innovation rate grows, required service level grows. S93
AttributeLow implied uncertaintyHigh implied uncertainty
Product marginLowHigh
Average forecast error10%40–100%
Average stockout rate1–2%10–40%
Forced season-end markdown0%10–25%

From S94.

  • Supply uncertainty: higher for new products, lower for mature ones (Hau L. Lee, 2002). S95
  • Spectrum: salt at a supermarket (predictable supply and demand) → existing automobile model → new communication device (highly uncertain). S96

Step 2: supply chain capabilities

  • Responsiveness = ability to respond to wide quantity ranges, meet short lead times, handle large variety, build innovative products, meet high service level, handle supply uncertainty. S97
  • Cost-responsiveness efficient frontier: more responsiveness costs more. S98
  • Responsiveness spectrum: integrated steel mill (highly efficient) → Hanes apparel (somewhat efficient) → most automotive production (somewhat responsive) → Dell (highly responsive). S99
Lecture 35S100–S133

Achieving Strategic Fit (contd.)

Step 3: achieve fit

  • Match responsiveness to implied uncertainty: high uncertainty → responsive, low → efficient. McMaster-Carr chooses a responsive chain for MRO variety and fast delivery. S102
  • Assign roles across stages: a retailer with little inventory passes uncertainty to a flexible manufacturer (England, Inc.). S103
  • Two extremes: efficient (Walmart) and responsive (Dell). There is no right SC strategy independent of competitive strategy, but there is a right one for a given competitive strategy. S105
EfficientResponsive
Primary goalLowest costQuick response
Product designMinimize product costModularity to allow postponement
PricingLower marginsHigher margins
ManufacturingHigh utilizationCapacity flexibility
InventoryMinimize inventoryBuffer inventory
Lead timeReduce, but not at greater costAggressively reduce even if costly
Supplier selectionCost and low qualitySpeed, flexibility, quality
TransportationLow-cost modesFast modes

Marshall L. Fisher, HBR 1997 S107.

  • Other issues: competitive changes over time, product life cycle, multiple products and segments. S108
  • Life cycle: early = uncertain demand, high margins, availability matters; late = predictable, low margins, price matters. The chain moves from responsive to efficient (pharma, Intel). S110 S114
  • Tailored supply chain: efficient where implied uncertainty is low, responsive where high. Dell (contract makers for Walmart channel), Levi Strauss (standard vs custom jeans), W.W. Grainger (fast movers decentralized, slow movers centralized). S112–S113

Intracompany interfunctionalMaximize company profit view. S117
Intercompany interfunctionalMaximize supply chain surplus view (e.g. joint promotions). S117
Agile (flexible) intercompanyKeep fit while partners change over time. S118
  • Challenges: growing product variety and shrinking life cycles (Apple limits variety), globalization (Honda's flexible plants), fragmented ownership, changing technology (Dell's move into retail), environment and sustainability (Starbucks). S121–S123
  • Walmart: strategic sourcing, long-term vendor alliances, cross-docking at the heart of replenishment; out-of-stocks down 16%. S124–S126
  • Dell: before 2007, direct sales, build-to-order, JIT, supplier integration; after 2007, retail distribution, diversification, lean, partnerships, sustainability. S127–S129
Lecture 36S134–S159

Make or Buy Decision

  • A strategic alliance is a legal agreement between two or more companies committing resources to common goals (new markets, IP, infrastructure, technology, people). S136
TypeCommitment · example S138–S140
Non-equity allianceLowest: no ownership shared. Two tech firms co-develop software.
Equity allianceModerate: one acquires equity in the other. A carmaker takes a stake in a battery firm.
Joint ventureHighest: a new co-owned legal entity (NewCo). Two pharma firms launch a drug.
  • Vertical complementary alliances: firms at different stages of the value chain. Horizontal: firms at the same stage, often competitors. S141–S142
  • Motives: domestic (market share, pooling resources, economies of scale, complementary resources); international (new markets, avoiding import barriers, international synergies, core competencies). S143
  • Outsourcing benefits: cost savings, specialized expertise, focus on core competencies, scalability, efficiency, risk sharing, customer focus. S147–S148
  • Limitations: loss of control, quality risk, communication challenges, dependency, data security, hidden costs, impact on internal workforce. S149–S150
Cost of making (in-house)Cost of buying (outsourcing) S151
Production, extra labour, monitoring, storage, waste disposalPurchase price, sales tax, shipping, inventory holding, ordering
  • Implementing make-or-buy: assess the need → analyse cost and expertise → evaluate quality and control → consider capacity and scalability → case study → tips. S153
  • Cases: Apple designs in the US, makes in China (manufacturing is not its core competency); Nike keeps design and marketing, uses contract manufacturers; Google core competencies in search algorithm, data analytics, UX design. S154–S156
