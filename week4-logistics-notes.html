Lecture 18S1–S29

Trends in Logistics

Covers: history, categories, stakeholders, trends and technologies, cold chain, blockchain, big data S2

History of logistics

  • Traced back to military contexts; computing advances in the 1960s and 1970s brought mathematical modelling into logistics. S3
  • Logistics became functional departments; third-party transport and LSPs professionalized; the idea of the "Industrialization of Services" took root. S3
  • UPS, FedEx and Walmart pioneered the "Industrialization of Logistics Services". Then came E-Logistics, and now RFID and the Internet of Things. S4

Categories of modern logistics (Behrends, 2016) 7 items

RetailChain retailing and small independent stores, each with its own supply and delivery pattern. S7
Consumer shopping tripsTrips to shops and carrying goods home; big share of freight energy use. S7
Courier, Express, Parcel (CEP)Global networks with cross-dock terminals and urban delivery tours. S7
HoReCaHotel, Restaurants, Catering: frequent Just-In-Time deliveries. S8
ConstructionMaterial-intensive; irregular but timely supplies to sites. S8
Waste managementSpecialized vehicles and pick-up tours to recycling and disposal sites. S9
Industrial and terminal haulagePart and unit loads from manufacturing districts and terminals. S9

Stakeholders of modern logistics (Behrends, 2016) 3 groups

  • Shippers and receivers: decide shipment size, frequency, lead time and precision, so they determine transport demand. S10
  • Carriers: provide transport services, own-account or third-party; aim for high resource utilization. S11
  • Public authorities: land-use and traffic planning, roads and rail; balance economic activity with residents' quality of life. S11

Modern concept 1: cold chain logistics

  • Managing and transporting temperature-sensitive products (perishables, pharmaceuticals) in a controlled environment, from refrigeration to freezing. S18
  • Key components: temperature control, packaging (insulated containers, refrigerated trucks), monitoring and tracking, risk management (temperature excursions), regulatory compliance (FDA, WHO). S18–S19

Modern concept 2: blockchain

  • A decentralized, distributed ledger that records transactions securely and immutably without intermediaries. S20
  • Elements: transparency and traceability, enhanced visibility, improved security (cryptographic encryption, decentralized consensus), streamlined processes (smart contracts: self-executing contracts with predefined rules), reduced costs and delays. S21–S22

Modern concept 3: big data

Roles: predictive analytics · route optimization · warehouse management · demand forecasting · supply chain visibility · risk management. S23–S25

Challenges for modern logistics 5 items

  • Supply chain disruptions: 2023 Canadian wildfires, California floods, Russia–Ukraine and Israel–Hamas wars, strikes, low water at the Panama Canal. S51

Lecture 20S66–S86

Logistics vs SCM

  • The terms are often used interchangeably; some call SCM the "new" logistics. What the US calls SCM is more commonly logistics management in Europe. S68
  • Inbound Logistics readers: "There isn't a difference today" (Wayne Johnson, American Gypsum); "SCM incorporates logistics, and logistics is a number of sub-processes within SCM" (Michael Kirby). S69
Logistics (CSCMP) · exact words
"part of the supply chain process that plans, implements and controls the efficient, effective forward and reverses flow and storage of goods, services and related information between the point of origin and the point of consumption in order to meet customer's requirements." S73
  • SCM (Michigan State, Supply Chain Logistics Management): collaboration between firms to connect suppliers, customers and partners; SCM decisions are strategic and set "the operational framework within which logistics is performed". S72
  • Logistics covers demand planning, inventory, transportation, fleet management, material handling, order fulfilment. Inbound: acquisition, handling, transport and storage of materials. Outbound: collection, maintenance, distribution to the end user. S74
  • Objective of logistics: desired product at the right time, quality, place and price. S74
  • What they share: both deal with goods, services or information; both raise customer satisfaction; both support competitive success; same flow from supplier to manufacturer to wholesaler to retailer. S75

Case: GEODIS and an online retailer

  • Problems: big seasonal swings, next-day or two-day delivery promise, accurate inventory. S82
  • Solutions: KPI monitoring, demand forecasting, scalable pop-up warehouses, shipping from warehouses nearest transport hubs. S82–S83
  • Result: a multi-country network and a 91% fulfilment rate for European customers. S83
