Lecture 42S1–S25

Importance of Distribution in Supply Chain

Distribution S3

The steps taken to move and store a product from the supplier stage to a customer stage. It is a key driver of profitability because it affects both supply chain cost and customer value. Walmart uses distribution to offer high availability of common products at very low cost.

  • Network design has two phases: (1) the broad structure, e.g. sell direct or through an intermediary; (2) specific locations, capabilities, capacities and demand allocation. S4

Service factors 7 items

Response time (short response → facilities close to customers) · product variety · product availability (probability of being in stock) · customer experience · time to market · order visibility · returnability. S5–S6

Cost factors

  • Changing the network affects inventories, transportation, facilities and handling, information. Sourcing and pricing also matter. S7
  • Shorter desired response time → more facilities needed. S8
  • More facilities → inventory costs rise S9; facility costs rise S12; transport costs fall until inbound economies of scale are lost, then rise (U-shape) S10.
  • Inbound transport = bringing material into a facility; outbound = sending it out. Outbound cost per unit is usually higher because inbound lots are larger (Amazon receives full truckloads of books, ships small parcels). S11

Design options

Direct shipping · centralized distribution · decentralized distribution · cross-docking · drop-shipping (suppliers ship directly to customers, no inventory held) · hybrid · 3PL providers. S14–S15

Online sales and distribution

  • Considerations: fulfilment centres, inventory management, last-mile delivery, order processing and packaging, returns, technology, customer experience, scalability, integration with stores (ship-from-store, in-store pickup), data analytics. S16–S18
  • Effect on service: physical products take longer (digital ones are instant); more variety (Netflix vs rental stores); better availability via aggregation; 24/7 access; faster time to market (Walmart.com); order visibility; returns are harder and costlier; direct sales; flexible pricing; efficient funds transfer. S19–S20
CostEffect of online sales S21–S23
InventoryLower via aggregation (Amazon vs Barnes & Noble) and postponement (Dell assembles after order; Amazon prints rare books on demand).
FacilitiesFewer facilities: Netflix ~50 warehouses vs Blockbuster's thousands of stores. But groceries cost more to handle online.

Lecture 43S26–S57

Designing Distribution Networks

Six distribution network designs S28

DesignHow it works · highlights
1. Manufacturer storage with direct shipping (drop-shipping)Manufacturer holds stock and ships to the customer; retailer passes orders on. Lowest inventory (aggregation), high transport cost, response time one to two weeks, easy variety, returns expensive. S29–S32
2. … with in-transit mergeParts from several sources are combined by the carrier so the customer gets one delivery (e.g. PC + monitor). Somewhat lower transport than drop-shipping; better customer experience. S33–S35
3. Distributor storage with carrier deliveryDistributor/retailer warehouses hold stock; package carriers deliver. Faster response, higher inventory than manufacturer storage. S36–S38
4. Distributor storage with last-mile deliveryDistributor delivers to the customer's home; very quick (same/next day), highest transport cost; grocery and e-commerce. S39–S42
5. Manufacturer/distributor storage with customer pickupCustomer collects from a pickup site: lower transport, limited convenience. S43–S45
6. Retail storage with customer pickupStock in local stores; same-day pickup, inspect before buying; highest inventory and facility costs, lowest transport. S46–S47
  • Selecting a design: customer needs, product characteristics (value, demand variability, perishability), costs, industry dynamics, scalability, ownership, overall strategy. S48
  • Suitability (S50): drop-shipping suits low and very-low demand, high-value, high-variety products (+2); retail pickup suits high-demand products and quick response (+2). Key: +2 very suitable … −2 very unsuitable. S50

Case: Dell's online sales

  • Dell pioneered online sales of computer hardware (about half the category by 2009) and began selling via Wal-Mart in 2007. S51
  • Benefits: customization, fast time to market, 24/7 low-cost service, negative working capital (paid before it pays suppliers), inventory reduction via aggregation and postponement. Drawback: higher outbound transport (single PCs shipped). S52–S53
  • Online works well for high-value customized PCs; less so for low-cost standardized PCs (response time −2, transport −2). S53–S54
  • Conclusion: hybrid online + offline models are common; Amazon and Blue Nile profited, Webvan struggled. S55
Lecture 44S58–S93

Facility Location Planning

  • The facility location problem (FLP, location analysis) is a branch of operations research and computational geometry: place facilities to minimize transport cost, considering e.g. keeping hazardous materials away from housing. S60
  • Location decisions are strategic: they affect the whole organization, last long, are difficult to reverse and capital intensive; new plants, expansion, decentralization and shutdowns are constantly reviewed. S60
  • If a location was poorly planned: sell it (disinvestment), close and liquidate, continue operating, or relocate. S61–S62
  • Three-tier model of location competitiveness: country (government rules, openness to trade, finance), sector (efficiency in management, competitive design and marketing), company (skilled labour, infrastructure, technology). S64

Factors affecting location 10 items

Electric power availability and cost · natural disasters · property tax · corporate income tax (single sales-factor apportionment is more favourable) · sales tax · labour availability and quality · construction cost · telecom infrastructure · economic development incentives · quality of life. S65–S66

International factors: economic, environmental, political and legal, social, technological, plus supply chain proximity, customers, flexibility, language, healthcare and education. S67–S69

Multi-facility strategies

By product/serviceP&G: separate plants for cleaning products and personal care. S70
By geographic areaAmazon: fulfilment centres for North America, Europe, Asia. S71
By processTesla: Nevada Gigafactory makes batteries; Fremont, California assembles vehicles. S72

Evaluating location alternatives

  • Preliminary screening: infrastructure, suppliers, costs, quality, free trade zones, political risk, business environment, customer proximity, government barriers. S73
  • Qualitative: simple comparison chart (e.g. Biratnagar vs Kathmandu on community attitude and lifestyle) and point rating (sum the scores; the slide picks B with 34 over A 33 and C 30). S74–S75
  • Factor rating: list factors → weight them → rating scale → score each site → weighted sum → choose the highest. S76–S78

Centre of gravity model

  • Cx = Σ(wixi) ÷ Σwi; Cy = Σ(wiyi) ÷ Σwi; weights are volumes shipped or customers served. S85–S86
  • Same data: x₀ = (10×452 + 30×678 + 80×483 + 50×711 + 80×539) ÷ 2,863 = 49.66; y₀ = 39.64. S87
  • Location examples: sugar mills near cane farms; tea factories in Assam, West Bengal, Karnataka; ADNOC in the UAE. S88

Mechanical analogue: Varignon's frame

  • Strings through holes in a table carry weights; the common knot P settles at the least-cost location (no friction). R₁…Rm = raw material sources, M₁…Mn = markets, m + n weights; uses Euclidean (straight-line) distance; weight = annual trips × cost per unit distance. S89–S90
