Lecture 21S1–S23

Fundamentals of Supply Chain Management

  • Example chain (detergent): timber → paper → packaging; oil company → chemicals and plastics → P&G → supermarket or 3rd-party DC → Big Bazaar → customer. S3
Supply chain · Chopra & Meindl (2013)
A supply chain consists of all parties involved, directly or indirectly, in fulfilling a customer request. It includes not only the manufacturer and suppliers, but also transporters, warehouses, retailers, and even customers themselves. S5

Within a firm it includes new product development, marketing, operations, distribution, finance and customer service. Dawei & Chen: a group of interconnected companies that add value to inputs from source to end product. S5

  • SCM combines demand and supply networks; each link tries to cut cost and improve long-run performance. S6
  • Three flows between manufacturer and customer: information, product, funds. S9
  • SCM network stakeholders: suppliers, manufacturers, warehouses and DCs, customers; costs at each link: material, transportation, manufacturing, inventory. S10
  • Objective: maximize the overall value generated. Listed aims include timely delivery, effective inventory, flexibility, cost-effective production, collaboration, optimized delivery, customer value, quality, integrated technology. S11

Lecture 22S24–S48

SC Operations: Manufacturing versus Service SC

  • SC operations: supplier sourcing, supplier-purchaser collaboration, supplier information management, purchase order management, inventory, warehouse and transport management, SC planning, shipping. S26
  • Four functions of operations management: planning, scheduling, control, improvement. S27
  • SCM is described as a subset of operations management, managing material and information flow among suppliers and customers. S28
  • Five unique attributes of services: intangibility, perishability, heterogeneity, inseparability, customer participation. Perishability mirrors inventory management; heterogeneity etc. have manufacturing counterparts like modularity and JIT. S29
  • Manufacturing SC: flow of raw materials, components and information through production, assembly, packaging and distribution. Example model: three-layer supplier–manufacturer–retailer model with inspection for defective items (Pal & Mahapatra, 2017). S31–S32
  • Service SC: delivering intangible services (healthcare, transport, hospitality, professional services); customer interaction is central. Supplier → service organization (supplier relationship, operation, customer relationship) → customer. S33–S34
  • Service SC framework: strategy balances competitive service requirements with cost and investments; Plan, Source, Deliver, Return (repair and recovery, disposition). S35

Sourcing management

Procurement management

  • Overseeing and administering all methods of acquiring materials, products and services; needs continuous monitoring, not one-off. S59
  • Fundamentals: understand your purchasing portfolio, organize purchases, control inventory, include procurement in product development, risk management and KPIs, customer needs, look outside your market, prioritize relationships, collect spend data, know your negotiating position. S60
  • Example: a construction company sourcing cement and steel for a building project. S62

Supply management

  • Identifying, acquiring and managing resources and suppliers essential to operations. Goals: cost control, efficient resource allocation, risk management, gathering information for decisions. S64
  • Role: source and procure per leaders' wishes; build and hold suppliers accountable; use technology; consider supply and demand theory. S65
Lecture 24S68–S100

Principle Streams: Operations, Materials, Production, Logistics, Distribution

Operations management

  • Optimizing operations to maximize efficiency, productivity and profit; converting inputs (materials, labour, equipment, technology) into outputs. S71
  • Managers handle process design, balance resources and costs, shape product design, oversee strategy, streamline workflows. S72
  • Key concepts: processes, capacity, utilisation, flow. Process: planning → scheduling → execution. S73–S74
  • Types of operations: manufacturing, service, retail, hospitality, logistics, supply chain. S75–S76
  • Strategies: reduce costs, improve quality, productivity, use data, set goals and forecast, collaboration, HRM, improve supply chain. S77

Materials management

  • Planning, organizing and controlling the flow of materials a manufacturer needs: from material requirement planning to sourcing, storage, control and distribution. Goal: right quantity, right time, right cost. S78
  • Primary objectives: low prices, high inventory turnover, low procurement cost, continuous supply, consistent quality, low payroll cost, good supplier relations. Secondary: reciprocal relations, new materials, make-or-buy, standardization, product improvement, interdepartmental harmony, forecasts. S79
  • Components: MRP (quantity and timing of raw materials), purchase planning (balance holding vs ordering cost), inventory control, material supply management, quality control. S80–S81
  • Significance: cost reduction, quality, timely delivery, inventory optimization, efficient supply chain. Challenges: SCM complexity, cost control, quality control, data management. S82 S84
  • Improves profitability via procurement, logistics, inventory carrying, waste and manufacturing costs, and lower working capital. S85

Production management

  • Elwood S. Buffa: decisions on the production process to make goods to specification, in the right amount, at the right time, within budget. S86
  • E.L. Brech: "the process of effective planning and regulating the operations of that section of an enterprise which is responsible for the actual transformation of materials into finished products" (criticized for ignoring the human factor). S86
  • Uses the 6 M's: Man, Machine, Methods, Money, Materials, Markets. S86
  • Types: intermittent (project, job shop, batch) and continuous (mass and flow, process). S87
  • Objectives: right quantity, quality, time and cost. Functions: product and resource selection, production planning and control, machine maintenance and replacement. S88–S89
  • Strategies: continuous improvement, JIT, TPM (total productive maintenance), SCM. S90

Logistics and distribution management

  • Logistics management: planning, implementing and controlling movement and storage of goods, services and information; right product, place, time and condition at minimum cost. S93
  • Benefits: higher profitability, better delivery productivity, lower operating expenses, customer satisfaction, better intermodal operations, advanced route planning. S94
  • Components of logistics management systems: WMS, TMS, DMS. TMS acts as a mediator between legacy order processing and warehouse distribution. S95–S96
  • Distribution management systems: automated order management, inventory allocation, warehouse forecasting. S97
  • Challenges in distribution: insufficient skilled resources, inadequate capital, disconnected technical infrastructure, multi-brand distributors, reluctance to modernize. S98
Lecture 25S101–S131

Objectives of Supply Chain Networks

  • Objective: maximize overall value generated. Supply chain profitability = what the customer pays minus the firm's cost to supply it; the higher it is, the more value generated. The only source of revenue is the customer. S103–S104
  • Other objectives: cost of quality, replenishment, speedy delivery, shorter order time, efficiency, delivery optimization, awareness, class performance, inventory optimization, demand fulfilment, flexibility, business characteristics, efficacy, cost reduction, better decisions, better distribution. S104–S109

Supply chain network design

  • A supply chain network is the interconnected system of organizations, facilities, activities and resources that produce and deliver goods or services. S111
  • CIPS: network design "covers all of the movements and storage of raw materials, work-in-process inventory, and finished goods from the point-of-origin to the point-of-consumption." S111
  • Design decisions: facility role, facility location, capacity allocation, market and supply allocation. S113

Factors influencing network decisions 8 items

StrategicCost leaders pick lowest-cost locations (Foxconn, Flextronics in China); responsive firms locate near markets (Zara in Portugal and Spain). S115
TechnologicalBig economies of scale → few high-capacity plants (semiconductors); low fixed cost → many local plants (Coca-Cola bottling). S116
MacroeconomicTaxes, tariffs, exchange rates, shipping costs. S117
Infrastructure1990s firms chose Shanghai, Tianjin, Guangzhou for infrastructure, not lowest cost. S117
PoliticalStability; Global Political Risk Index (GPRI) by Eurasia Group: government, society, security, economy. S118
CompetitiveLocate near or far from competitors. S119
Customer response timeConvenience stores: many, close to customers; supermarkets: fewer, larger. S120
Logistics and facility costsMore facilities → inventory and facility costs rise, transport costs fall until inbound economies of scale are lost. S121
Phase I · SC strategyBroad design: stages, in-house vs outsourced, from the competitive strategy; forecast global competition; capital constraints. S123
Phase II · Regional configurationRegions, roles, approximate capacity. Homogeneous needs → large consolidated facilities; varied needs → smaller local ones. Big economies of scale → few plants (e.g. AMD). S124
Phase III · Desirable sitesHard infrastructure: suppliers, transport, communication, utilities, warehousing. Soft infrastructure: skilled workforce, turnover, community receptivity. S125
Phase IV · Location choicesPrecise location and capacity for each facility to maximize total profit. S126

Case: Zara (Inditex)

  • 2009: about €11 billion sales, 4,700+ outlets in about 76 countries. Design-to-sales cycle 4–6 weeks vs 6+ months for the industry; new designs every week; 75% of display changed every 3–4 weeks; about half the markdowns of competitors. S127
  • Flexible sources in Europe (Portugal, Spain) and low-cost sources in Asia; Inditex owns about 40% of capacity. Uncertain-demand products from Europe, predictable ones from Asia. Over 40% of purchases after the season starts (vs under 20% typically). S128
  • Eight DCs in Spain; delivery within 24 hours in Europe, up to 48 hours to America or Asia; several shipments a week. S129
