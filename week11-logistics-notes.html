Lecture 52S1–S25

Inventory Management

  • Inventory: all items, goods and materials a business holds to sell for profit: an asset (tangible or intangible) that can be realized for revenue, or is in process for sale. In manufacturing it includes raw materials and WIP; in services it is mostly intangible (steps before a sale). S3

Types of inventory 8 items

Raw materialsItems processed into the product; often perishable (shelf-life chemicals). S7
WIPWork in process: short conversion to finished goods. Work in progress: construction and services, cost vs % completion. S8
Finished goodsReady for sale; one firm's finished goods can be another's raw material (nuts and bolts for generators). S9
MROMaintenance, Repair and Operating supplies: lubricants, gloves, bulbs; not counted as inventory in the books. S10
BufferPrevention is better than cure; sometimes for demand surges, while safety stock covers supply delays. S11
CycleOrdered in lot sizes regularly to meet minimum production needs. S12
DecouplingReserve stock so one machine's breakdown doesn't stop the line. S13
Transit (pipeline)Shipped but not yet received. FOB shipping: ownership passes to the buyer once goods are loaded. S14

Inventory planning

  • Determining the optimal stock at any time to meet demand: how much and when to order. S15
  • Process: data analysis → demand forecasting → supplier planning (cost, timeliness, quality, reputation) → safety stock → storage space analysis → monitoring → watch innovations. S16–S18
  • Safety stock = (max daily sales × max lead time) − (average daily sales × average lead time). S17
  • Advantages: optimized levels, better cash flow, satisfied customers. Challenges: disruptions, shifting demand, limited cycle counts, multi-channel warehousing. S19–S20
Lecture 53S26–S61

Inventory Management (contd.)

Common methods: JIT, MRP, EOQ, DSI. S28

MethodIdea · examples · watch-outs
JITJapan, 1960s–70s, Toyota credited most; receive goods only as needed. Toyota, Dell, Harley-Davidson. Risky if demand spikes or one input is late. S29–S32
MRPSales-forecast dependent; combines production schedule, inventory and bill of materials (BOM). Ski maker example; Toyota, Intel, Boeing. Needs accurate data; complex. S33–S36
EOQOrder size minimizing total holding + setup cost, assuming constant demand. Assumes fixed costs and no shortages. S37–S41
DSIDays to turn inventory into sales; also called average age of inventory, DIO, days in inventory. Lower is generally better. Pfizer, Ford, Nestlé. S42–S46
  • DSI = average inventory ÷ (COGS ÷ 365); average inventory = (beginning + ending) ÷ 2. S43
  • Zero inventory (zero inventory system/manufacturing): JIT-based lean idea. Working principle: demand-driven, small batches, continuous improvement, supplier collaboration, pull system. Examples: custom-made shirts; marketplaces like Amazon that connect buyers and sellers. Challenges: volatility, supplier dependence, logistics reliability, coordination, contingency plans, systems, data accuracy. S47–S51
  • VMI: the supplier manages inventory at the customer's warehouse using regularly shared stock and consumption data. Customer gains: no stock-outs, lower logistics cost, fewer admin tasks. S52–S53

ABC analysis uses the Pareto (80/20) principle and is often combined with JIT. S54–S55 Products can be stored; services cannot, so service firms manage queues, not inventory. S56

Lecture 54S62–S92

Supply Chain Digitalization

  • Digitization: replacing manual or analog processes with digital solutions to streamline operations and increase visibility. S64
EraDevelopment S65–S68
Late 20th centuryComputerization; ERP centralizes data (but lacks real-time agility)
1990sInternet, e-commerce, EDI
Early 2000sWMS, TMS, demand planning tools
Mid-2000sBig data, cloud, predictive analytics, ML, AI
RecentIoT sensors for real-time tracking
NextBlockchain, robotics, autonomous vehicles
  • Transformation enablers: e-commerce integration, cloud computing, AI, IoT, blockchain, supply chain digital twins. S69
  • Key components: big data analytics, IoT, cloud, blockchain, digital twins, RPA (robotic process automation), visibility platforms, collaboration platforms, cybersecurity, network optimization, predictive maintenance, real-time track and trace, supplier platforms, customer engagement, sustainability, risk management, compliance, continuous improvement. S70–S74
  • 5 steps: define a vision (assess legacy systems, data, skills) → unify data and processes → automate planning (not complex collaborative planning) → use data and analytics → align people with processes. S75–S77
  • Benefits: financial management, shorter lead times, better decisions, automation, organizational flexibility, innovation, end-to-end customer engagement. S78–S79
  • Factors fostering digitalization: government initiatives, market demand and competition, technological advancements, globalization and complexity. S80–S82
  • Challenges: information systems and data (the main challenge, technical), team training (PwC 2023: two-thirds of decision-makers expect skills must improve), change management. S83–S85
  • Healthcare: 77% prefer online appointments, 47% want to know more about their doctor, 38% want to check hospital facilities; telemedicine, wearables, 3D imaging. S86
  • Dell (Borella et al., 2021): founded 1984; direct model; moved to built-to-stock PCs in 2012; started digital transformation in 2017; levers: actors, processes, technologies, guided by five stakeholder experiences. S87–S89
  • Main benefit: greater visibility → better decisions, lower inventory, more time to act. S90
Lecture 55S93–S121

Smart Supply Chains

  • Roots: early 20th-century scientific management and assembly lines (Frederick Taylor, Henry Ford) → globalization → ERP in the 1980s–90s → smart technologies and Industry 4.0 (cyber-physical systems, cloud, AI). S95–S99
  • Definitions are inconsistent; framed by smart drivers (smart logistics, smart warehouse), data streams, and technologies (IoT, big data). Characteristics: instrumented, interconnected, intelligent, automated, integrated, innovative, adaptable. S100–S101
  • Hierarchical framework (Zhang et al., 2023): Level 0 technologies (ICT, AI/ML) → Level 1 smart functions → Level 2 smart network (responsiveness, efficiency, resilience, flexibility, sustainability) → Level 3 smart business / Industry 4.0 → economy, environment, society. S102
TraditionalSmart S103
TransparencyLimitedFull, via smart tools
Data collectionMay be lost or distortedReal-time via sensors and IoT
ResponsivenessSlowQuick
TraceabilityLimited and costlyIntelligent tracing of all operations
AdaptabilityVulnerable to changeResponds to sudden market changes
FeedbackSlow, delayedReal-time at all stages
  • Key components: sustainable practices, agile and resilient, responsiveness, efficiency, flexibility, real-time visibility, predictive analytics, automated processes, dynamic optimization, collaborative networks. S104–S108
  • Efficiency = ability to produce without wasting materials, time or energy, expressed as 0–100%; in manufacturing, actual ÷ expected output. S111
  • Sustainable SC closes the loop of upstream and downstream flows (recycling, recovery) and covers economic, environmental and social goals. Three Ps: Profit, People, Planet. S113–S114
  • Implementations: IoT-based, blockchain-based, cloud and AI-based sustainable smart SCs; smart power grids with Industry 4.0. S115–S118
Lecture 56S122–S154

Robust and Resilient Supply Chain Design

RiskOperational, financial or strategic; manage with redundancy, diverse suppliers, contingency plans. S125
VulnerabilitySusceptibility to disruptions. Wagner and Bode (2006): a function of supply chain characteristics. S126 S143
RobustnessAbility to withstand and recover while keeping essential functions; flexibility, redundancy, alternative sourcing. S126
ResilienceThe ultimate goal: bounce back and learn from disruptions. S127
  • SCRM process (Dell life cycle): define → assess → mitigate → monitor & report, in a feedback loop across physical, cyber and human threats. S128
  • External risks: demand, supply, environmental, business (e.g. a supplier being sold). Internal: manufacturing, business, planning and control, mitigation and contingency. S129–S131

SCRM strategies 7 items

  1. PPRR model: Prevention, Preparedness, Response, Recovery. S133
  2. Manage environmental risk (COVID: multi-sourcing; breweries made sanitizer, Ford made ventilators). S134
  3. Track freight carrier metrics: transit time, number of stops and stop time, loading time, route optimization, maintenance; keep logistics contingency plans. S135–S137
  4. Internal risk awareness training. S138
  5. Monitor risk continuously. S138
  6. Model risk scenarios with data. S139
  7. Consolidate data in one repository. S139
Temple of Supply Chain Resilience S140–S141

Foundation: effective supply chain strategy. Floor: product design for SCM. Four pillars: agility, collaboration, SCRM culture, supply chain design. Roof: transparency. Crown: continuous monitoring and intelligence.

  • Resilience strategies (3SC): diversify suppliers, resilient inventory, advanced technology, strong supplier relationships, map risks, strengthen transport, sustainability. S142
  • Vulnerability drivers (Zhang et al., 2023): supply-side risk, operation process risk, demand-side risk, environmental risk (disasters, tariffs, exchange rates). S144
  • Robustness aspects: redundancy, flexibility, resilience, visibility, collaboration, risk management, technology. Components: demand planning, supplier relationships, inventory, transport, IT. S145–S147
  • JD.com's self-operated mode (~60% of sales) buys from suppliers and runs inventory, delivery and after-sales. Integration gives high collaboration, effective information sharing and agility, so better resilience and less bullwhip. S148–S151
Lecture 57S155–S186

21st Century Supply Chain

  • Global chains became more interconnected and complex: efficient in stable times but highly vulnerable to disruption. S158
  • Key aspects: digitalization and automation; visibility and transparency; sustainability and CSR; globalization and outsourcing; customer-centricity and omni-channel; resilience. S158–S161

Supply chain trends in 2024

  1. Integral visibility: real-time visibility moves from "nice to have" to "must have". S163
  2. Sustainability as a brand differentiator (ESG disclosures, Paris Agreement). S164
  3. Regionalized supply chains: nearshoring, multi-node networks, dual sourcing. S166–S167
  4. Robotic and autonomous systems (labour shortages, 5G). S168
  5. Next-generation AI (incl. generative AI) for predictive chains. S169
  • AI benefits (Imaginovation): predictive maintenance cuts downtime 35–45%; smart quality control raises production 20% and quality 35%. McKinsey: AI forecasting cuts errors by up to 50%. S170
  • Logistics evolution: technology integration, e-commerce boom (last mile, drones). Challenges: disruptions, sustainability, labour shortages. Solutions: blockchain, predictive analytics, collaborative platforms. S171–S174
  • ESG (Environmental, Social, Governance): measure performance, manage risk, improve transparency; challenge is diverse regulations and perceived costs. S175–S176
  • WBSCM (Web Based Supply Chain Management): an internet-based commodity ordering, acquisition, distribution and tracking system supporting food aid and nutrition programs and US commodity marketing. S177–S178

Case of India (CII Conclave, 11 Sept 2020)

  • Logistics industry USD 215 billion, growing at 10.5% CAGR; ~75% of companies worldwide hit by COVID disruptions. S180
  • Logistics cost 14% of GDP vs global ~8% → competitiveness gap of USD 180 billion, rising to USD 500 billion by 2030. S181
  • Vision 2030: cut logistics cost to 7–8% of GDP, optimize modal mix, digitize 90% of SC operations, omni-channel, green chains, skills. S181
  • Barriers: modal mix road 64%, rail 30%, waterways 5%, air 1%; indirect costs 4× developed countries (inventory carrying USD 120–180 bn); freight trains at 24–25 km/h; low tech adoption; fragmented trucking; retail ~90% unorganized, 8% organized, 2% e-commerce. S182

Worked example (my own numbers, using the slide formulas)

D = 1,000 units/year, S = ₹50 per order, H = ₹4/unit/year → EOQ = √(2 × 50 × 1,000 ÷ 4) = √25,000 ≈ 158 units. Max daily sales 20 × max lead time 10 = 200; average 15 × 7 = 105 → safety stock = 95 units.
