Lecture 45S1–S45

Demand Forecasting

Indian demand context

  • World Bank India Development Update (Oct 2023): India grew 7.2% in FY22/23, second highest in the G20, almost twice the emerging-market average; bank credit growth 15.8%; FY23/24 forecast 6.3% (services 7.4%, investment 8.9%). S3
  • Q3 FY2024: real GDP 8.4%; private investment +10.6%; private consumption 3.5%; government consumption −3.2%. S4
  • GVA grew 6.5% (manufacturing 11.6%, construction 9.5%, services 7%, agriculture −0.8%). GDP growing faster than GVA means demand outpacing supply, a possible source of inflation. S5
Demand forecasting S7

Understanding and predicting customer demand to make decisions on supply chain operations, margins, cash flow, capex and capacity planning, often using historical data; it helps avoid over- and under-production.

  • Five influencing factors: seasonality, competition, geography, economy, types of goods (perishables need precise forecasts; subscriptions are predictable). S8–S9
  • Advantages: inventory management, cost reduction, customer satisfaction, production planning, resource allocation, marketing, financial planning, risk mitigation, strategic decisions, SC optimization, seasonal planning, competitive advantage. S10–S12
  • Challenges: lack of data, over-reliance on past data, limited visibility, inaccurate models, poor processes, insufficient resources, unpredictable external factors. S13–S14

PassiveUses only past sales; for firms aiming at steady sales.
ActiveFor startups and fast-growing firms; includes growth plans and competition.

Techniques

Qualitative: Delphi, salesforce composite, expert/insider knowledge, surveys, interviews/focus groups. Quantitative: moving averages, trend projection, seasonal averages, econometric models, machine learning. S17

  • Delphi: panel of experts (typically 10–50) answer questionnaires independently (avoids groupthink); summaries fed back; repeated for 2–4 rounds until consensus. S18–S19
  • Salesforce composite: combine CRM data, several methods with weights, and sales reps' input. S20–S21
  • Surveys: online, telephone, face-to-face (high response, costly), mail (low response). S24–S26

Capacity Planning

  • Capacity planning balances available resources with demand; capacity = work completed in a given time. S48
  • By horizon: long-term/strategic (several years), medium-term/tactical (months to a year), short-term/operational (days to months, reactive). S49
  • By resource: workforce, product, tool, resource, project capacity planning. S53–S54
Design capacityMaximum output under ideal conditions. S50
Effective (system) capacityDesign capacity minus allowances (maintenance, breaks, scheduling). S50–S51
Actual outputCannot exceed effective capacity; hit by breakdowns, absenteeism, shortages, demand. S50
EfficiencyActual output ÷ effective capacity × 100 S51
UtilizationActual output ÷ design capacity × 100 S51
Example 1 S52

230 units/hour, 7 days × 3 shifts × 8 h → design capacity = 168 × 230 = 38,640/week. Effective 28,000; actual 25,000. Utilization = 25,000 ÷ 38,640 = 64.7%; efficiency = 25,000 ÷ 28,000 = 89.3%.

  • Process: estimate requirements → evaluate existing capacity and gaps → identify alternatives → financial analysis → qualitative issues → choose the best long-term option → implement → monitor. S55–S57
  • Alternatives: add capacity, optimize existing, outsource, flexible workforce, demand management, contingency plans, Industry 4.0. S56
  • Demand patterns: growth, decline, cyclical, stable. S58
  • Evaluating alternatives: economic (break-even analysis) and non-economic (public opinion, employee and community reaction). S59

Break-even analysis

TC = FC + VC; VC = Q × v; TR = Q × r; P = Q(r − v) − FC; QBEP = FC ÷ (r − v). S60–S61

ExampleWorking
Pizza: FC $4,000/month, v $4, r $9 S62BEP = 4,000 ÷ 5 = 800 pizzas. Profit $10,000 needs (10,000 + 4,000) ÷ 5 = 2,800. Demand of 700 < 800 → not profitable.
Make or buy (ABX): make FC $160,000, v $100; buy $150/unit S63–S65Q = FC ÷ (VB − VM) = 160,000 ÷ 50 = 3,200. At 1,000 units: make $260,000 vs buy $150,000 → buy. Make is cheaper for Q > 3,200.

Sequencing & Scheduling

  • Sequencing: the order of processing tasks on resources. Scheduling: sequencing plus start and finish times. S71

Sequencing rules on one example

Jobs A–E: processing 3, 4, 2, 6, 1 days; due 5, 6, 7, 9, 2. S73

  • SPT gives the lowest total and mean flow time; LPT aims to cut changeovers and raise machine utilization; EDD targets deadlines. S75–S77
  • Least slack (LS): slack s = d − p. Jobs (p, d): J1 (4, 10), J2 (3, 8), J3 (5, 12), J4 (2, 7), J5 (1, 5) → slacks 6, 5, 7, 5, 4 → sequence J5, J2, J4, J1, J3. S78–S79
  • Critical ratio = time remaining until due date ÷ remaining processing time; lowest first; combines EDD and SPT; dynamic. A 10/5 = 2.0, B 15/3 = 5.0, C 5/2 = 2.5, D 7/7 = 1.0 → D, A, C, B. S80–S82
  • Advantages: avoid bottlenecks, adaptable, lean, archive know-how. Challenges: dynamic changes, limited resources, complexity, conflicting priorities, setup times, technology limits. S83–S84

Scheduling and Johnson's rule

  • Single-machine scheduling: n jobs, one operation. Flow shop: n jobs, m operations, same sequence. General flow shop is exponential; Johnson's rule solves 2 machines, n jobs optimally in polynomial time. S85–S86
  • Rule: pick the shortest time; if on machine 1 schedule the job first, if on machine 2 schedule it last; remove it; repeat. Conditions: constant times, independent of sequence, equal priority, all jobs go M1 then M2. S87
  • Example (M1, M2): A (3.2, 4.2), B (4.7, 1.5), C (2.2, 5.0), D (5.8, 4.0), E (3.1, 2.8) → C → A → D → E → B. S88–S90
  • Three machines: if min(A) ≥ max(B) or min(C) ≥ max(B), replace with G = A + B and H = B + C and apply Johnson's rule. S91

Transportation Management

  • Four major components of transportation decisions: flow, mode, load, route. S100
  • Load: quantity and composition of goods per trip. Aspects: size, weight (regulations, fines), volume (palletization, stacking), packaging, composition (consolidation), balancing (axle weight, centre of gravity). S101–S104
  • Fleet size: transport demand, service levels (SLAs), geography, cargo characteristics, redundancy, total cost of ownership, technology (telematics), regulation, scalability. S105–S107
  • Mode choice factors: transit time, cost, reliability, distance, safety, environment. S110
ModeBest for · drawback S111–S116
RoadDoor-to-door, short-medium distance, last mile, JIT · congestion, higher cost per mile long-haul
RailLong distance, bulk, heavy, energy-efficient · limited access, extra handling
MaritimeBulk, long-haul, lowest cost per ton, global trade · slow, port congestion
AirTime-sensitive, high-value, perishable · highest cost, limited bulk
PipelineLiquids, gases; low energy · high initial investment, limited cargo types
IntermodalCombines modes; door-to-door · coordination and documentation
  • Logistics flows: physical, information, financial, reverse. Route planning weighs distance, time, cost, capacity, mode, infrastructure, regulation. S119–S123
  • US firms spend over $600 billion a year on transportation and about $1 billion on TMS (software that automates carrier selection, load building, routing, scheduling, freight audit). S125
  • TMS examples: Polaroid (HighJump), Tree of Life inbound freight efficiency 55% → 75% (JDA), Orange Glo (LeanLogistics). ARC Advisory Group: TMS value now spans other departments and partners. S126
Lecture 49S129–S151

Transportation Problem: Basics and North-West Corner

  • A special linear programming problem: ship one commodity from m sources to n destinations at minimum total cost, meeting supply and demand. S131 S134
  • Assumptions: fixed supply and demand, single commodity, linear costs, route capacity, non-negative variables, full shipment. S132–S133
  • Balanced: total supply = total demand. Unbalanced: add a dummy source or destination. S138
  • Phase I: initial basic feasible solution (NWCR, LCM, VAM). Phase II: optimal solution (MODI). A non-degenerate solution has m + n − 1 allocations. S139–S141
  • NWCR: start at the top-left cell, allocate min(supply, demand), strike the satisfied row or column, move on. Simple but may not be optimal. S142–S143

Lecture 50S152–S193

Transportation Problem: Least Cost, VAM, MODI

  • Least Cost Method: allocate to the cheapest cell first. Same data: Jaipur–Delhi (cost 1) gets 40 first. Cost = 1×40 + 3×40 + 3×20 + 6×30 + 2×40 = Rs 480. A tie at cost 3 gives an alternate solution of Rs 520. S154–S162
  • Vogel's Approximation Method (VAM), the penalty method: for each row and column, penalty = difference between the two lowest costs; pick the largest penalty; allocate to its cheapest cell; repeat. Here also Rs 480; VAM is generally the most effective of the three. S163–S167

MODI (Modified Distribution) optimality test

  • Needs m + n − 1 allocations in independent cells. S172
  • For occupied cells set ui + vj = cij (take one variable = 0). For empty cells compute dij = cij − (ui + vj); u + v is the "implicit cost". S174–S177
  • All dij positive → optimal; a zero → optimal with alternate solutions; any negative → improve via a closed loop (+θ/−θ). S178
  • In the LCM solution: u₃ = 0 → v₂ = 2, v₁ = 6, u₁ = −5, v₃ = 6, u₂ = −3; all vacant cells positive → Rs 480 is optimal. S175–S178

Unbalanced and second example

  • Supply 117 > demand 95 → add a dummy demand column of 22 with zero costs. If demand exceeded supply, add a dummy supply row. S179–S182
  • Example 2 (P, Q, R × A–E; Σ = 340): LCM gives Rs 1,410; MODI finds d₁₁ = −1, so one loop of θ = 10 improves it to the optimum Rs 1,400, with an alternate optimum (d₃₂ = 0). S183–S189
  • Advantages: competitive advantage, cost minimization, service levels, inventory management, resource optimization, time efficiency. S190–S191
Lecture 51S194–S221

Assignment Problem Programming

  • A special case of the transportation problem: assign n resources to n activities one-to-one to minimize cost or maximize profit (workers to machines, salesmen to areas). S196–S197
  • LP form: xij = 1 if resource i does task j, else 0; each task gets exactly one resource and each resource one task. S198–S199
  • Balanced: square matrix. Unbalanced: rows ≠ columns → add a dummy row or column with zero cost. S200–S201

Hungarian method

  1. Balance the cost table. S201
  2. Row reduction (subtract each row's minimum), then column reduction. S201
  3. Assign single zeros by rows then columns; strike other zeros in the same row/column. S201–S202
  4. Optimal if assignments = number of rows. S202
  5. Otherwise cover all zeros with the minimum number of lines (tick method). S202–S203
  6. Subtract the smallest uncovered value K from uncovered cells, add K at line intersections. S203
  7. Repeat until optimal. S203
ExampleResult
Balanced: 3 programmers × programmes A, B, C (minutes) S205–S2071 → C (80), 2 → B (90), 3 → A (100); total 280 minutes
Unbalanced: 4 jobs × 5 machines (dummy job 5) S208–S2121 → B (3), 2 → A (10), 3 → D (1), 4 → C (6); machine E gets no job; total Rs 20
  • Advantages: efficiency, clarity of objective, ease (binary variables, tables), flexibility, real-time applications (IoT, workforce, routing). S213–S217
  • Assumptions: deterministic demand and supply, homogeneous tasks and resources, fixed costs, no transshipment, linear relationships, simple capacity limits, single period, non-negative variables, fixed origins and destinations. S218–S219
